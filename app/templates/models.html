The models behind it

Four AI steps turn a recording into notes. We trained one of them ourselves and measured all the results below on lectures the models never saw during training.

What each model does

  1. 1 · Speech → text

    Whisper small · OpenAI

    Listens to the audio and writes down every word, with punctuation. Detects the language by itself; for non-English speech it also produces an English version.

    Pretrained · runs on this computer
  2. 2 · Text → subject

    Subject classifier trained by us

    Reads the transcript and picks one of 8 subjects, so the lecture can be filed automatically. Shows the words behind each decision.

    Trained on {{ '{:,}'.format(baseline.sizes.train) if baseline else '—' }} lectures · results below
  3. 3 · Long text → summary

    BART large-CNN · Meta

    Rewrites text in fewer words. It reads at most ~700 words at once, so the transcript is cut into ~500-word parts, each part is summarized and the results are joined.

    Pretrained on news articles · runs on this computer
  4. 4 · Summary → your language

    Google Translate

    Translates the finished summary into one of 14 languages, on request.

    Online service · only the summary text is sent

Training the subject classifier

Data. {{ '{:,}'.format(baseline.sizes.train + baseline.sizes.valid + baseline.sizes.test) }} real academic lectures from the TIB dataset, each with a transcript and a subject label. We kept lectures with exactly one subject from 8 subjects.

Fair testing. The lectures were split 70 / 15 / 15: the models learn from the first part, settings are tuned on the second, and the scores below come from the last {{ '{:,}'.format(baseline.sizes.test) }} lectures, which the models never saw.

Why not just accuracy? 85% of the test lectures are Computer Science, so a model that always answers “Computer Science” already scores 85%. Macro-F1 scores every subject equally, so that trick gets 0.12.

Finding: the simpler model won

DistilBERT is a neural network that already understood English; we fine-tuned it for this task. It finds rare subjects more often (recall {{ '%.2f'|format(d.recall) }} vs {{ '%.2f'|format(t.recall) }}) but raises more false alarms (precision {{ '%.2f'|format(d.precision) }} vs {{ '%.2f'|format(t.precision) }}), mostly calling Computer Science talks “Information Science”. Two reasons: we told it to pay extra attention to rare subjects, and it reads only the first ~2,000 words of a lecture, while the word-count model reads 3,000. Subject is mostly a matter of keywords, which word counting captures well.

So the app uses the word-count model: more accurate, 4 MB instead of 250 MB, and instant. Open any summary and click “Why …?” under its title to see the words behind its decision.
